Opportunities

The case for keeping the internet open rests on something more substantive than abstract principle. The empirical record shows that free online communication has changed what citizens can actually do politically, not just what they can say. From coordinating protests to, in one genuinely unprecedented recent case, selecting a national government through a gaming app, social media has given citizens organisational capabilities that did not exist before. This page examines that evidence critically, including where the opportunities are real and where they have been overstated.

What Social Media Actually Does During Protests

Jost et al. (2018) conducted one of the most thorough analyses of social media's role in political protest, studying movements across Turkey, Ukraine, Spain, and the United States. Their central finding is worth stating directly: social media's contribution is less about raising awareness and more about solving the practical coordination problems that prevent collective action from happening at all. They identify three distinct mechanisms.

Real-Time Logistics

During Turkey's 2013 Gezi Park protests, mainstream Turkish media largely ignored what was happening. Twitter became the channel through which protesters learned where police were positioned, where medical help was available, and which blood types were urgently needed at hospitals. Over 30 million protest-related tweets were sent in the first month. This is not symbolic participation. It is the kind of real-time information sharing that determines whether people can safely attend a protest, whether the injured get help, and whether participants know when to disperse. None of this was possible at scale before social media (Jost et al., 2018).

Building the Sense That Others Agree

Research on Occupy Wall Street found that social identification with a movement, meaning a genuine felt connection to other participants and their cause, was the single strongest predictor of whether a Twitter user physically attended demonstrations (Jost et al., 2018). This matters because collective action problems are largely psychological. People who support a cause often do not act on it because they underestimate how many others share their views. Social media creates visible evidence of shared sentiment, which lowers the perceived risk of participation and raises the perceived likelihood that collective action will succeed.

Why Casual Sharers Matter More Than Expected

A common criticism of online political participation is that sharing content without attending protests achieves nothing meaningful. Jost et al. (2018) tested this assumption directly by modelling what would happen if the outermost, least-active layers of the Turkish protest network were removed. Total audience reach dropped by 50%. The core organisers generated most of the original content, but casual sharers were responsible for half the network's total reach. The critique of so-called slacktivism confuses individual contribution with collective effect. The two are not the same.

Ukraine's Euromaidan Protests (2014)

Ukraine's 2014 Euromaidan protests offer a particularly well-documented case because researchers tracked social media activity against specific offline events throughout the crisis. The picture that emerges is of platforms being used not as supplements to traditional organising but as its primary infrastructure (Jost et al., 2018).

Infrastructure Built in Days

The main EuroMaydan Facebook page was created on November 21, 2013. Within two weeks it had 125,000 likes. By the end of the crisis it had accumulated 600,000 comments, 2.2 million shares, and 7.9 million likes. Separate pages were created to coordinate carpools from across Ukraine, organise volunteer medical care, and provide legal support for those arrested (Jost et al., 2018). A survey of protesters found 40% said friends or family on Facebook encouraged their participation. That is not a peripheral finding. It describes a primary recruitment mechanism.

Online Activity Closely Tracked Offline Events

One of the stronger pieces of evidence from the Ukrainian case is that spikes in social media activity corresponded precisely to major offline events: the initial surge in late November, clashes after anti-protest laws passed on January 16, 2014, and a sharp rise following police violence that killed 20 protesters on February 18 (Jost et al., 2018). The number of Twitter accounts in Ukraine more than doubled between July 2013 and July 2014. This correlation between platform growth and protest intensity suggests that people were joining specifically to participate, not because social media was generally becoming more popular at the time.

Nepal 2025: Social Media as Electoral Infrastructure

The Nepal case of September 2025 represents a qualitatively different use of social media than protest coordination. As Neo, Higgs and Floridi (2025) document, it is the first recorded instance of a social media platform being used to select a national head of government. This warrants serious analysis both as evidence of what open platforms make possible and as a test case for the limits of that possibility.

How the Discord Election Unfolded

When Prime Minister KP Sharma Oli resigned on September 9, 2025 following protests sparked by the government's own social media ban, a political vacuum formed. The military, holding de facto control, approached organisers of the Youth Against Corruption Discord channel and requested a candidate nomination. Hami Nepal, the civic organisation running the channel, hosted a live debate among five candidates streamed to around 16,000 viewers. A subsequent poll collected 7,713 votes, with former Chief Justice Sushila Karki receiving 49.7% (Koirala, 2025; Neo, Higgs and Floridi, 2025).

The Result Carried Real Political Weight

The military submitted Karki's name, the President invoked emergency constitutional powers, and she was sworn in on September 12, 2025 (Neo, Higgs and Floridi, 2025). She subsequently dissolved parliament, formed a cabinet, and established a judicial inquiry commission. The Discord channel grew to 145,000 members within four days. What is significant here is not simply that this happened but that it worked. A platform designed for gaming communities became, under conditions of state collapse, a recognised source of political authority. That is a remarkable demonstration of what open digital infrastructure can enable when official channels fail entirely.

The Broader Shift in Political Organisation

These cases reflect a structural change in how collective political action is organised. Baecker (2019) notes the contrast between the 1963 March on Washington, which required nine months of planning to assemble 250,000 people, and the 2017 Women's March, where a small number of Facebook posts produced 3.5 million participants internationally. Obama's 2008 campaign recruited 5 million volunteers and contributions from 3.1 million individuals, figures that would have been operationally impossible without digital tools. What the evidence from Jost et al. (2018), Baecker (2019), and Neo, Higgs and Floridi (2025) suggests collectively is that open internet access does not merely make political participation easier. It changes who can participate and at what cost, which has structural implications for how power operates in democratic societies. Censorship, on this reading, is not only a restriction on speech. It is an intervention in the distribution of political power.
