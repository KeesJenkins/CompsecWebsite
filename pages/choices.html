Choices

The evidence reviewed across this site does not point toward a single correct approach to online censorship. It points toward a set of genuinely difficult choices that governments, technology companies, and individuals make differently, with different consequences. This page examines those choices critically, arguing that the central question is not whether to regulate online speech but rather who should make those decisions and with what accountability. As Baecker (2019) put it: can harmful content be blocked while free speech is preserved? The difficulty of that question is not a reason to avoid answering it.

Government Regulatory Approaches

Different legal frameworks reflect fundamentally different assumptions about the relationship between state authority and individual expression. Comparing them reveals that no single model has resolved the core tension between protecting citizens from harm and protecting their right to speak (Dumbrava, 2021).

Three Contrasting Models

The European Union's Digital Services Act requires large platforms to remove illegal content, publish transparency reports on algorithmic systems, and conduct risk assessments for systemic harms including electoral interference, while maintaining broad protections for lawful speech. The United States relies primarily on Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, which shields platforms from liability for user-generated content and leaves moderation largely to corporate discretion. Authoritarian models use national security and anti-misinformation legislation to justify restrictions that in practice suppress political opposition (Dumbrava, 2021). Each model faces legitimate criticism. The EU approach risks over-regulation that chills legitimate speech; the US model has produced platforms that accept no responsibility for the harms they enable; and authoritarian models have no credible accountability mechanism at all.

The Lesson From Nepal's Shutdown

Nepal's September 2025 decision to ban over 24 social media platforms illustrates the practical failure of internet shutdown as a governance tool. The ban was framed as enforcement of registration requirements, but Koirala (2025) documents that it was widely understood as an attempt to silence government critics. Rather than suppressing opposition, it drove activists to Discord, where they built the infrastructure for a national election. The UN Human Rights Council has declared deliberate internet shutdowns a violation of international human rights law, but this has not prevented governments from using them. The Nepal case adds empirical weight to that position: shutdowns demonstrably fail even on their own terms of preserving political stability.

Platform Choices and the Problem of Accountability

Technology companies make consequential decisions every day about what speech is permitted, amplified, or removed. The difficulty is that they make these decisions without the democratic legitimacy of elected governments and without the legal accountability of regulated industries (Dumbrava, 2021).

Moderation Errors Cut Both Ways

Baecker (2019) documents cases illustrating the difficulty of getting content moderation right at scale. Facebook suspended a user for non-derogatory use of the word "dyke". Twitter removed Rose McGowan's account while she was speaking publicly about Harvey Weinstein's abuse. YouTube's removal of extremist content inadvertently deleted thousands of videos documenting Syrian atrocities that human rights organisations had been working to preserve. These are not edge cases. They reflect a structural problem: moderation systems optimised for scale will produce systematic errors, and those errors affect real people with real stakes in the outcomes. The question is not whether platforms can achieve perfect moderation but whether the harms of imperfect moderation are distributed fairly and whether there is any meaningful recourse.

Warning Labels as a Case Study in Failure

Blanchar and Norris (2024) provide perhaps the most methodologically rigorous test of a widely adopted platform intervention. Their experiment found that Twitter's disputed tags on election misinformation were ineffective across the board and counterproductive for politically knowledgeable Trump voters, among whom exposure to the tags was associated with stronger belief in the false claims. This is a significant finding because disputed and false information labels represent the dominant platform response to disinformation. If this approach does not work and may backfire, it raises the question of what platforms are actually achieving with their moderation policies beyond signalling effort. Dumbrava (2021) argues that platforms consistently prioritise engagement over accuracy, and that this structural incentive cannot be corrected through voluntary commitments alone.

The Market Access Question

Google's 2010 withdrawal from China after refusing to operate a censored search engine is frequently cited as an example of principled corporate behaviour. Baecker (2019) provides the context that complicates this reading: by 2006, at least 60 people were in Chinese prisons for content they had posted online, and Microsoft, Amazon, and other major companies had already established compliant Chinese operations. Google's withdrawal was the exception, not the norm. Dumbrava (2021) argues that industry-wide minimum standards for non-cooperation with censorship demands could change this calculus, but acknowledges that no enforcement mechanism currently exists. Former Google counsel Daphne Keller argued that concentrating content decisions in private companies is dangerous, but that government control creates different and potentially greater risks (cited in Baecker, 2019). Both positions have merit, which is why neither has prevailed.

Individual Choices

While structural and regulatory choices determine the overall environment, individuals also make decisions that collectively shape how online censorship operates in practice. Dumbrava (2021) identifies digital literacy and circumvention tools as the two most practically significant categories.

Digital Literacy as a Countermeasure

Research cited by Dumbrava (2021) found that proactively prompting users to consider the accuracy of content before sharing it improved the quality of what they subsequently shared, without requiring any specific correction or platform intervention. This prebunking approach works because it appeals to people's existing sense of responsibility for their own claims rather than positioning the platform as an authority correcting their errors. The finding matters because it suggests individual choices about how to engage with information, informed by a clearer understanding of how recommendation algorithms work and who benefits from engagement-maximising content, have measurable effects on information quality.

Circumvention Tools and Their Limits

VPNs, the Tor browser, and end-to-end encrypted messaging give individuals meaningful capacity to access censored content and communicate privately. Nepal's activists demonstrated this: when mainstream platforms were banned, they migrated to Discord and maintained the communication infrastructure that eventually enabled a national election (Neo, Higgs and Floridi, 2025). The limitation of this picture is significant. Circumvention tools are most reliably available to people who are already urban, educated, and technically literate. Those most exposed to government censorship, in rural areas of highly restrictive states, are typically the least equipped to use these tools. Individual choices can make a meaningful difference, but they do not resolve the structural inequalities that make censorship effective in the first place.

Who Should Own Democratic Infrastructure?

The Nepal Discord election raises a question that sits at the intersection of all three levels of choice discussed above. The election was hosted on a platform owned by a US corporation, subject to US jurisdiction, governed by Discord's own Terms of Service rather than Nepali law. Discord could theoretically have suspended the channel or altered its polling functions at any point, and Nepali authorities would have had no legal recourse. Neo, Higgs and Floridi (2025) call this corporate sovereignty over democratic processes, and argue that it represents a new category of risk that existing frameworks for thinking about online censorship do not adequately address. The question is not simply whether governments should censor the internet. It is whether the infrastructure through which democratic deliberation increasingly takes place should be owned and controlled by a small number of private corporations based in one country, subject to the commercial and legal priorities of that country alone. Dumbrava (2021) arrives at a similar conclusion from the regulation side, arguing that platforms require genuine, independently enforced accountability obligations rather than voluntary commitments, because the business model that drives their design is structurally indifferent to the democratic harms it produces. Neither the Nepal activists nor any regulatory body has fully resolved this problem. It remains the central unresolved question in online censorship.
