Technology / Topic

Online censorship sits at the intersection of politics, technology, and human rights, and it is becoming more relevant, not less. Governments have always tried to control what people can say. What changed with the internet is the scale of the problem on both sides: more speech, and more sophisticated tools for suppressing it. This page examines how those suppression tools actually work, why corporate complicity has made them more effective than they would otherwise be, and why treating China and North Korea as extreme outliers is a mistake.

How Censorship Works Technically

The popular image of online censorship is a government blocking a website. In practice, the systems involved are considerably more layered. China's Great Firewall, the most thoroughly documented example, combines several methods at once, each targeting a different point in the communication chain (Gisonna, 2025). Understanding how they work together matters because it explains why circumvention is difficult and why no single technical fix is enough.

Filtering and Blocking Methods

  • Keyword filtering scans content in real time for prohibited terms and prevents pages from loading if a match is found
  • DNS poisoning intercepts requests for banned domains and either returns no result or redirects users to an approved page instead
  • IP address blocking cuts off access at the network level to specific servers regardless of what content they host

Deeper Traffic Inspection

  • Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) reads the actual contents of data packets crossing the network, not merely their destination addresses, allowing detection of encrypted traffic patterns
  • TCP reset attacks terminate active connections without the user receiving any explanation, making the experience feel like a technical failure rather than censorship
  • All companies operating within China are legally required to self-censor their public content or risk losing market access entirely

China's Great Firewall

China's censorship system began in 1998 as the Golden Shield Project under the Ministry of Public Security, and it has been refined steadily ever since (Gisonna, 2025). Today it blocks Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, Twitter, and major international news outlets. Rather than simply restricting the internet, China has effectively built a parallel one, directing users toward domestic alternatives that operate under government supervision: WeChat for messaging, Baidu for search, and Bilibili for video. This distinction matters. China has not failed to control the internet; it has succeeded in replacing it.

Corporate Complicity

One of the more uncomfortable findings in the literature is how readily Western technology companies have accommodated Chinese censorship requirements. Microsoft operates a filtered version of Bing within China, and both Amazon and Google have established compliant local services (Gisonna, 2025). Google did withdraw in 2010 after refusing to filter search results, and activists gathered outside its Beijing office to protest, but this was an exception rather than a norm. Baecker (2019) notes that by 2006, at least 60 people were in Chinese prisons for posting material the government classified as sensitive. The commercial logic of market access has, in most cases, outweighed principled objections to state censorship.

The Tendency to Expand

Hong Kong and Macau historically maintained significantly lower levels of internet censorship than the mainland. Following national security legislation passed in June 2020, experts warned that this would change (Gisonna, 2025). This pattern is worth paying attention to. Censorship infrastructure, once built and legally justified, tends to extend its reach. The technical capacity developed to suppress one category of content rarely remains limited to that category for long. What begins as targeted restriction tends over time to become broader control.

North Korea: Isolation Rather Than Filtering

Comparing China and North Korea reveals that state control of online information takes fundamentally different forms depending on a government's starting assumptions. China filters the global internet while remaining partially connected to it. North Korea, by contrast, has largely opted out. Deibert et al. (2008) described the country as a virtual black hole in cyberspace, operating an isolated domestic intranet called Kwangmyong comprising roughly 30 government-approved websites available only to a small privileged minority. This is not a less sophisticated approach to censorship; it is a different approach entirely.

Access as a Privilege, Not a Right

Content on Kwangmyong comes from state agencies including the Central Scientific and Technological Information Agency. Access is not publicly available. Only government ministries and a handful of approved institutions have the equipment and authorisation to use the network (Deibert et al., 2008). The few public intranet cafes that existed in cities like Chungjin charged approximately 20,000 won per month, seven to eight times the average monthly wage. That pricing is not incidental. It is the censorship mechanism, using economic exclusion to achieve the same outcome that China achieves through technical filtering.

Constitutional Rights Without Enforcement

Article 67 of the DPRK's Socialist Constitution formally guarantees freedom of speech and of the press. Deibert et al. (2008) note that no legal mechanism exists through which citizens could challenge the state's control over online access. Personal radios and televisions must be modified to receive only government broadcasts and registered with authorities. Mobile phones were banned nationwide from May 2004. The North Korean case illustrates a broader point: constitutional guarantees of free expression mean very little unless there are independent institutions capable of enforcing them against the state itself.

A Global Problem, Not an Authoritarian Outlier

It is tempting to frame online censorship as something authoritarian governments do and democratic ones do not. The data challenges this. Freedom House (2021) found that approximately 75% of global internet users, around 2.8 billion people, live in countries where someone has been arrested for content posted online (cited in Dumbrava, 2021). Access Now documented over 180 deliberate internet shutdowns in 2022 alone, used primarily to disrupt protests and elections. In 2017, Pakistan arrested dozens for social media posts; Turkey banned thousands of websites including Wikipedia; Egypt systematically filtered local news and human rights content (Baecker, 2019). Baecker (2019) argues that no society treats free speech as absolute, and every legal system carves out exceptions for obscenity, defamation, and incitement. The meaningful debate is not whether online speech should ever be restricted, but who gets to make that call, under what rules, and with what accountability.
