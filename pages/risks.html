Risks

The risks associated with online censorship do not all point in the same direction. Government surveillance and internet shutdowns suppress legitimate speech and political participation. At the same time, unmoderated platforms enable the rapid spread of disinformation and political manipulation. Even attempts to solve these problems, such as platform content moderation and warning labels, carry their own risks. The evidence suggests that there is no cost-free position in this debate: every approach involves trade-offs that deserve critical scrutiny.

Surveillance and the Chilling Effect

One of the less obvious mechanisms through which online censorship operates is the chilling effect: the tendency of people to modify or suppress their own political expression once they know or suspect that what they say is being monitored. Dumbrava (2021) identifies this as one of the primary social media risks to democracy, distinct from direct censorship because it requires no arrests and leaves no visible trace.

Evidence of Behavioural Change Under Surveillance

Following the 2013 revelations about US National Security Agency mass surveillance programmes, researchers tracked a measurable and statistically significant drop in traffic to privacy-sensitive Wikipedia articles covering subjects such as Al Qaeda, certain political movements, and related topics (Dumbrava, 2021). People had not been told their searches were monitored. They had simply learned that such monitoring was possible. That distinction is important: it demonstrates that the chilling effect does not require confirmed surveillance, only the credible possibility of it. Freedom House (2021) documented arrests for online speech in 56 countries and deliberate social media blocks by governments in 21 others, most commonly timed to coincide with protests and elections (cited in Dumbrava, 2021).

Shutdowns Often Produce the Opposite of Their Intended Effect

Governments increasingly use internet shutdowns as a first response to political unrest. Access Now tracked over 180 such shutdowns in 2022 alone. The Nepal case of September 2025 offers a particularly instructive example. The government banned over 24 social media platforms ostensibly to enforce registration requirements, but the ban was widely interpreted as an attempt to silence political criticism (Koirala, 2025). The four-day period between the ban's implementation and the escalation of protests proved decisive: activists migrated to Discord and established the digital infrastructure that would later be used to run a national election (Neo, Higgs and Floridi, 2025). The shutdown did not suppress dissent. It redirected it somewhere harder to monitor.

Disinformation: Scale, Reach, and the Limits of Correction

The research on online disinformation presents a genuinely complicated picture. The scale of exposure is alarming, but the evidence on actual impact is more contested than public debate typically acknowledges. Both facts matter for assessing the risks.

The Scale Is Real, Even if the Impact Is Contested

Russian troll accounts reached an estimated 126 million Facebook users during the 2016 US presidential campaign. The 20 best-performing fake news stories in that cycle received 8.7 million Facebook interactions in the final three months, more than the 19 largest legitimate news outlets combined (Baecker, 2019). A 2019 Oxford Internet Institute report found evidence of organised computational propaganda in 81 countries (cited in Dumbrava, 2021). These are large numbers. However, Dumbrava (2021) notes that researchers have consistently found exposure to disinformation to be heavily skewed toward those already highly polarised, and that the actual persuasive effect on voting behaviour remains difficult to demonstrate. The reach and the impact are not the same thing, and conflating them overstates what disinformation campaigns actually achieve.

Warning Labels Have Not Worked as Hoped

Blanchar and Norris (2024) ran a controlled experiment testing whether Twitter's disputed tags on Trump's 2020 election fraud posts changed what people believed. For Trump voters with high political knowledge, the tags were counterproductive: participants in the tagged condition were more likely to judge the false claims as true than those who saw the same posts without tags. The authors suggest the tags may have functioned as a signal that the mainstream was attempting to suppress something, which increased rather than reduced perceived credibility. For Biden voters, the tags had no meaningful effect in either direction. This finding has significant implications for platform moderation strategy, because it suggests that the most widely adopted soft moderation tool may not only fail to reduce misinformation but may actively reinforce it among the audiences most likely to encounter and believe it.

Filter Bubbles and Algorithmic Polarisation

The argument that social media traps users in personalised information bubbles has become widely accepted, but the empirical literature is more cautious. The effect appears to be real, measurable in some contexts, and likely exacerbated by bots, but considerably less total than popular accounts suggest (Dumbrava, 2021).

What the Data Actually Shows

A study of 10 million US Facebook users found that conservative users were exposed to 29% cross-cutting content, meaning content from sources that did not align with their existing views, compared to the 40% they would have encountered under random selection (Baecker, 2019). Facebook's recommendation algorithm drives over 70% of YouTube viewing time. These effects are real. Dumbrava (2021) notes, however, that social media is not most people's only source of information, and that the personalisation effect is less severe than initially feared. The more accurate claim is that algorithms amplify existing tendencies toward selective exposure rather than creating those tendencies from scratch.

Automated Amplification Makes Polarisation Worse

Analysis of political Twitter conversation in the period before the 2016 US election found that approximately one-fifth of all content was generated by around 400,000 bot accounts, automated programmes designed to appear as human users (Baecker, 2019). Bot accounts tend to be the initial high-volume spreaders of false information, which is then propagated by genuine users who do not know where the content originated. A New York University report concluded that while social media may not have caused political polarisation in the United States, the platforms have demonstrably intensified it (cited in Dumbrava, 2021). The distinction between causation and amplification matters for policy, but it does not reduce the practical significance of the problem.

Risks in Social Media Electoral Processes

Nepal's 2025 Discord election demonstrates what is possible when social media platforms substitute for state electoral functions. Neo, Higgs and Floridi (2025) argue that while this represented a democratic opportunity in that specific context, it also exposed structural vulnerabilities that would be unacceptable in any process claiming genuine democratic legitimacy.

The Participation Gap

The Discord election collected 7,713 votes. Nepal has approximately 30 million people, which means 0.025% of the population participated. Rural internet penetration in Nepal is around 17%, compared to 79% in urban centres. The electorate was structurally skewed toward participants who were urban, young, male, and digitally literate before a single vote was cast (Neo, Higgs and Floridi, 2025). Accepting those 7,713 votes as a binding national mandate implicitly replaces universal suffrage with a form of technocratic census suffrage in which the right to participate is contingent on geography, income, and technical access rather than citizenship alone.

Security Weaknesses and Platform Sovereignty

Investigators found that the Discord polls lacked geo-blocking, meaning non-Nepali citizens could participate (Neo, Higgs and Floridi, 2025). Discord permits multiple accounts, making the one-person-one-vote principle unenforceable. Bot accounts could have influenced outcomes cheaply and at scale. Crucially, Discord is a US corporation subject to US jurisdiction and governed by its own Terms of Service rather than Nepali law. Nepali authorities had no legal recourse over any decision the platform made about the channel. The election worked because the military chose to accept the result, not because the process met any recognised democratic standard. Neo, Higgs and Floridi (2025) describe this as corporate sovereignty over democratic processes, a risk that extends well beyond the Nepal case.
